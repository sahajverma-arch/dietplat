# Practical Adherence Rules

## Familiar foods, most days

A plan a client will actually follow leans on dishes they already recognize and are used to eating, with an occasional less-common dish for variety — not the reverse. A week dominated by unfamiliar or exotic-sounding dishes is harder to stick to, however nutritionally sound it is on paper.

## Realistic prep effort

A dish that requires unusually long or involved preparation doesn't belong as an everyday item in a working household's weekly plan — the everyday backbone of a plan should be dishes with ordinary, manageable prep, with anything more elaborate reserved for occasional inclusion at most.

## Avoid unnecessary elimination

A plan that avoids a food group or ingredient without a real clinical or preference-based reason to do so is harder to sustain and doesn't help adherence — restriction should be purposeful (an allergy, a genuine dislike, a clinical flag), not incidental to how the plan happened to be generated.

## Consistency a client can actually recognize week to week

A plan that looks completely different from one week to the next, even when the client's targets haven't changed, makes it harder for them to build a routine around it — some deliberate continuity in the kinds of dishes used week over week supports adherence better than constant reinvention.
