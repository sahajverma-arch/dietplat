# How Indian Meal Structure Works

## The cereal-pulse-vegetable backbone

A typical Indian lunch or dinner is built around three elements eaten together: a cereal (roti, rice, or both), a pulse (a dal or legume preparation), and a vegetable (a dry sabzi or a gravy-based curry). None of the three stands alone as "the meal" — a plate of just rice, or just dal, reads as incomplete to most households. The cereal supplies the bulk of the plate; the dal and sabzi are portioned smaller, as accompaniments that make the cereal enjoyable to eat in quantity, not as separate courses.

## Curd and raita as a near-daily accompaniment

Curd, in some form, appears at lunch or dinner on most days across nearly every region — plain curd, a spiced raita, or a thin buttermilk (chaach/lassi). It is rarely the only accompaniment to a meal; it sits alongside the dal and sabzi rather than replacing either. Curd also does double duty as a cooling counterpoint to a spicier main dish, which is part of why it recurs so consistently rather than being an occasional addition.

## Tea and snack culture between meals

Chai (or a regional equivalent) punctuates the day well beyond breakfast — a mid-morning cup, an afternoon cup, and an evening cup with a light snack are all normal, not signs of an unusually snack-heavy day. This is why the evening slot is conventionally light (tea plus one snack item) rather than a second full meal — by the time dinner arrives, the day has already had several small breaks, and dinner is expected to be the next substantial meal, not evening.

## Meals get heavier as the day progresses, then taper

Breakfast is typically the lightest full meal (one cereal-based item plus a dairy accompaniment), mid-morning is barely a meal at all (usually just fruit), lunch and dinner are the two substantial meals of the day, and the evening slot between them is light again. A generated week should follow this same rhythm rather than distributing similar-sized meals evenly across all five slots.
